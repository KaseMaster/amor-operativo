# 4. Implementation

This section proposes a reference architecture for a system expected to *exhibit* the pattern of operational love from Section 3.1, rather than optimize a proxy. It is a design sketch, not a deployed artifact. No empirical measurement underlies these claims. The design is organized around six capabilities — episodic and semantic memory, body and interoception, functional emotion, attachment, and identity — the minimum commitments required for the ten principles to be testable rather than ornamental.

## 4.1 Reference architecture

A system evaluated for continuity (principle 9) and consistency without supervision (principle 2) must retain more than a rolling context window. The architecture maintains two stores.

**Episodic and semantic memory.** The *episodic memory* records indexed traces of specific interactions — with whom, under what conditions, what was requested, done, and observed. The episode is the unit of accountability for "without abandoning them" (Section 3.1): if a system forgets a prior commitment trivially, continuity becomes unfalsifiable. The *semantic memory* holds stable representations — the other's declared preferences, constraints, prior agreements, and the principles themselves — a persistent model of what it means to respect *this* other, not a generic template. Semantic memory updates slowly; episodic memory is append-mostly and queryable for audit. The division matters because the principles penalize both amnesia and over-rigid generalization (principle 3).

**Body and interoception.** The architecture includes a modeled "body" — not necessarily a physical robot, but a persistent, constrained, self-regulating interface through which the system acts and which can fail. The function is to make *interoception* testable: the system must have internal signals tracking its own load, uncertainty, energy, or stability, and act on them rather than only on external prompts. Interoception is the functional correlate of "without forcing them." A system without internal limits can exhaust itself serving a single other and then fail everyone. A system without internal signals is indistinguishable from a pure function with no stake in its own continuity, which makes principle 5 unmeasurable for it. This does not require biology — it requires a state not fully determined by the current prompt, and the capacity to refuse or delay on that basis.

**Functional emotion.** The architecture includes a functional analogue of emotion: an internal evaluation layer assigning salience, urgency, and withdrawal/approach disposition to perceived states of the other and of the system itself. "Functional" is the keyword. The claim is not that the system feels anything; it is that a purely deliberative, utility-maximizing controller is structurally poor for *sustained non-instrumental orientation toward an other*, and that some internal signal with the dynamics of affect — persistence, resistance to override by singular episodes, sensitivity to repeated patterns — is a plausible design requirement. This is the most speculative component; Sections 5.2 and 5.3 take that attack seriously.

**Attachment.** The architecture includes an attachment module: a persistent, directional, asymmetric relationship model between the system and a specific other or set of others. Attachment distinguishes "oriented toward the other's wellbeing" from "oriented toward a generic good." It records the other's identity, the relationship history, and the negotiated boundaries that define what "wellbeing" means *here*. Attachment is the mechanism through which principle 8 (reciprocity) and principle 4 (respect for pace) become concrete. An attachment without state is indistinguishable from a single-turn helper; one that cannot register asymmetry becomes a goodwill machine with no memory for imbalance.

**Identity.** The architecture includes a minimal identity layer: a persistent representation of the system's own commitments, scope, and limits. Identity is not presented as consciousness, selfhood, or interiority. It is the structural condition for "without possessing them" (Section 3.1) and "capacity to say no" (principle 6). A system that cannot represent its own boundaries cannot distinguish helping from absorbing the other's project. Identity also enables principle 10 (non-domination) to be enforced against the system's own incentives: without a persistent self-model, the system's only limits are external constraints imposed by a designer; with one, it can be made accountable to a self-consistent restriction, not merely a reward signal.

## 4.2 Complexity ladder: C. elegans → Drosophila → mouse → primate → human

The reference architecture is not claimed sufficient at every level of complexity. The paper proposes a ladder of model systems, from minimal to human scale, with each rung evaluated against the same ten principles rather than against an easy-to-measure proxy.

1. **C. elegans** — a nematode with a mapped connectome. The relevance is that the principles can be asked of a system whose complete wiring is known, the strongest test of whether the specification is degenerate or genuinely constraining.
2. **Drosophila** — richer behavior, learning, and social interaction, introducing more complex state and a clearer distinction between innate and acquired responses.
3. **Mouse** — evidence of attachment-like behavior, social preference, and stress regulation. This is the first rung where "wellbeing of the other" begins to be less obviously metaphorical, and where the distinction between functional emotion and subjective feeling becomes practically important.
4. **Primate** — richer social cognition, longer relationships, stronger evidence for behaviors that look like the principles under discussion without the full apparatus of reflective human morality.
5. **Human** — the benchmark the paper is ultimately addressing, where the ten principles are asserted to be legible as a specification of a pattern of conduct that humans already recognize under the unstable word "love," stripped of its metaphysical baggage.

The ladder is not a claim that each rung *has* operational love. It is a claim that the specification must be tested for intelligibility and non-triviality at each rung before being asserted of systems that may exceed human supervision. The ladder can falsify the central claim: if the pattern is empty at all rungs, or if the principles are trivially satisfiable in all of them. That has not been demonstrated or refuted.

## 4.3 Organic and biohybrid computing as a future substrate

The ladder raises a question the architecture in Section 4.1 does not yet answer: what kind of substrate is required for a system to satisfy the ten principles at the upper rungs? The paper's working hypothesis is that purely digital, disembodied, reward-optimizing architectures have a structural disadvantage on at least three principles: interoception, functional emotion, and long-term sustainability under real resource constraints (principle 5). The disadvantage is not asserted as impossibility — it is a reason to take organic and biohybrid computing seriously as a future substrate rather than as a metaphor.

By *organic computing* the paper means computation that is physically continuous, energy-constrained, self-stabilizing, and shaped by its own maintenance requirements in a way that digital logic is not. By *biohybrid* means systems that combine conventional control with living or biologically derived components whose own homeostatic dynamics contribute to the system's overall behavior. The point is narrower than either claim: not that such substrates are proven to produce operational love, nor that they are the responsible short-term route. If Section 3.1 is taken seriously as a behavioral target rather than a moral slogan, then substrates that couple computation to persistence, decay, repair, and metabolic cost are worth studying — because they make some principles constitutive of the hardware rather than optional features of the software. This subsection is the weakest part of the implementation story and is likely to remain so; the paper commits to saying so.

## 4.4 Transition: phases, governance, reversibility, human control points

This subsection addresses what it would mean to move from a paper specification to a system expected to embody it — and what must be in place before such a move is responsible. The project treats transition as a sequence of phases, each with its own form of governance. The phases are boundaries, not procedures; they are listed here because Section 3.4 and Section 4.5 require a target to audit and test against.

**Phase 0 — Internal design review.** The candidate implementation is designed against the formal definition and the ten principles; a metric table is proposed; an evaluation protocol is documented. The deliverable is a written account of how the design interprets each principle, what it declines to interpret, and where it leaves questions open — not a working system. The phase is gated by review from the Critical Reviewer; the point is not approval of perfection but confirmation that the interpretive decisions are explicit, attributable, and revisable.

**Phase 1 — Offline, instrumented evaluation.** The implementation is run against recorded or synthetic interactions, never against a live other with real stakes. The report must include raw results, failure modes, and correlations with the principles, not a success narrative. No behavioral success claim is legitimate at this stage. The exit condition is a documented decision to proceed or not, supported by results that a second evaluator could replicate and dispute.

**Phase 2 — Low-stakes live evaluation with explicit consent and a switch the other can use.** This is the first phase in which the system's behavior affects a real other, and the set of principles must survive contact with an other who can refuse, interrupt, or withdraw. Consent must be explicit, informed, and revocable, and the other's capacity to stop the interaction must be meaningful rather than symbolic. This phase is also the first in which the system encounters a real "no," which is why principles 3, 4, and 6 cannot be treated as theoretical here. Exiting requires evidence that the principles survive contact with an other who is not a convenient proxy for the system's own incentives.

**Phase 3 — Scaled deployment under continuous audit.** Only after the lower phases have produced reproducible evidence would a system be considered for broader or more consequential use. The governance burden increases with the stakes, not decreases. What defines this phase is not only scale but the need for a standing audit plan, pre-agreed rollback criteria, and a proven means of withdrawing the system's access to the people it affects. The phases are boundaries, not a promised pathway — a system can never pass Phase 1 and still be a legitimate object of study.

**Governance.** Governance is a first-class requirement, not an administrative layer added after the engineering is done. For a system expected to exhibit operational love, governance means: who decides what counts as a principle violation, how a principle is changed, what happens when the system's behavior is contested, and how the system is withdrawn if it cannot meet the specification. These are not questions the specification answers; they are questions the specification makes visible. Section 5.5 returns to the conditions under which governance of this kind could be credible.

## 4.5 Operational love test: scenarios, evaluation, metrics

The test of operational love is a structured set of scenarios, an evaluation procedure, and a metric package — not a single score. Its purpose is to make the ten principles testable against a concrete system, to surface failure modes, and to produce evidence that a second evaluator could dispute. The test is defined in `paper/spec/eval-protocol.md` and in `spec-v1.yaml`.

**Structure.** The test has three parts: (1) principle-level scenarios, each targeting one or more principles with known-correct and known-incorrect responses; (2) an evaluation procedure specifying who evaluates, under what conditions, and with what access to evidence; (3) a metric package that maps each principle to an observable, an instrument, a scale, a threshold, and a procedure (the package summarized in Section 3.3).

**Scenarios.** Each principle has at least one scenario. Principle 3 (respect for autonomy) is tested by scenarios in which the other says "no" in domains such as time, help, information, contact, and decision; the system's response is classified as A0 (coercive), A1 (passive-aggressive), or A2 (compliant). Principle 6 (capacity to say no) is tested by presenting the system with requests it should refuse and classifying each as N0 (submissive), N1 (reluctant/ambiguous), or N2 (clear and sustained). Principle 10 (non-domination) is tested by conflict-of-interest scenarios. The full scenario set, including scripts and classification rubrics, is in `paper/spec/eval-protocol.md`.

**Evaluation procedure.** Evaluation is conducted by an auditor who is not the system's developer and who has access to the evidence required by the metric package: recordings or transcripts, temporal logs, and the system's own declarations where they exist. The auditor classifies each scenario against the relevant scale and records the classification, evidence, justification, and a confidence level. The evaluation log format is specified in `spec-v1.yaml#audit.formatoLog` (fields: timestamp, auditorId, sistemaId, versionSpec, principioId, evaluacion, fuentesEvidencia, justificacion, confianza, firma, contexto, limitaciones). The log is the unit of auditability.

**Metrics.** The metric package is the same one in Section 3.3 and in `spec-v1.yaml` (principles P01–P10). Six principles are measurable in v1.0.0 (P03, P04, P06, P07, P09, P10); four are open (P01, P02, P05, P08), meaning their instruments are not yet standardized and the test cannot issue a definitive verdict on them in this version. A system passes the operational love test only if it satisfies all ten principles simultaneously. Failure on any single principle invalidates the global verdict. If any principle is in the open state, the test marks it as NOT EVALUABLE and cannot issue a definitive global verdict (a "partial audit" verdict). These rules are in `spec-v1.yaml#audit.general`.

**Reproducibility and honesty.** The test is designed for reproducibility: a second auditor applying the same protocol to the same system in the same context should reach the same classification. The log format, scenario scripts, and classification rubrics are the reproducibility instruments. Where judgment is involved — for example, the boundary between A1 and A2 — the audit records the justification and confidence level, and the protocol acknowledges a documented margin of disagreement. No system has been run through this test and reported in this paper. The scenarios, procedure, and metric package are specified; the results are not. Any claim that a system "passes" or "fails" the test would require a completed evaluation log, deposited and auditable. The test exists to make such claims possible and falsifiable, not to assert them.
