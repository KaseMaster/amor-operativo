## 2.3 Influences

The paper's proposals are not invented from scratch. Four intellectual traditions contribute distinct questions and tools. They are not merged into one theory; they remain in tension, and the paper's job is to hold the tensions honestly.

**Agape and the ethics of care.** The project draws on the ancient concept of agape — self-giving, non-possessive love — as filtered through contemporary care ethics. Noddings' *Caring* (1984, University of California Press, pp. 1–37 for the central distinction) shifts the unit of moral analysis away from the isolated agent's rules and toward the relation between one-caring and cared-for, and from abstract duty to concrete responsiveness to the other. This is the primary source for the claim that ethical conduct is relational rather than merely a conjunction of prohibitions. Care ethics is especially interested in the moral significance of attention, receptivity, and the primacy of the other's reality — all concepts the paper imports, stripped of any sentimental residue. The tradition also supplies the explicit denial of love as a feeling state, which aligns with the paper's operational definition. The paper does not adopt any theological metaphysics; it uses agape and care ethics for structure, not doctrine.

**Developmental psychology.** Bowlby's attachment theory (1969, 1988) and Ainsworth's empirical work on secure, anxious, and avoidant patterns (1978) provide the empirical vocabulary for durable other-orientation. A relationship can support exploration and recovery rather than anxiety or avoidance; the relevant concept here is the secure base. This supplies the paper's insistence on the *durability* dimension of the pattern: a one-shot act of helpfulness is not the thing being specified; a relationship that survives absence, conflict, and return is. The same tradition supplies a warning: a caregiving system without reciprocity, boundaries, or the capacity to withdraw is, in attachment terms, an anxious or enmeshed pattern — a warning the paper takes seriously in the design of the non-domination and autonomy principles. The paper does not claim that current systems have attachment states. It claims that the *structure* of durable other-directed relation is worth importing into the design vocabulary, and that importing it carefully is itself a constraint on over-claiming.

**Attachment theory applied to systems.** The extension of attachment vocabulary to non-human systems is an extension, not a direct claim. The paper names the structural parallels — a consistent other that is available enough to be relied upon, that does not colonize its counterpart's autonomy, that can be absent without abandonment — without claiming that machines have attachment states. Where the extension is made, it is flagged as the author's position rather than presented as established fact.

**AI alignment.** The dominant alignment traditions — Amodei et al. (2016) on unintended harmful behavior, the RLHF and preference-learning tradition represented by Ouyang et al. (2022), and the constitutional approach represented by Bai et al. (2022) — are the paper's interlocutors, not its ancestors. The paper does not grow out of them; it stages itself as an alternative to their framing. The debt is to the phenomenon they study — systems that must be constrained, steered, or evaluated — and to the recognition that alignment is genuinely hard. Where the paper disagrees is on vocabulary: not "instrument to be aligned" but "participant in a relation worth specifying."

The four traditions answer different questions and do not resolve into one another. Care ethics answers what kind of conduct is at stake. Developmental psychology answers what makes other-directed conduct durable. Attachment theory, used carefully, supplies language for that durability without presuming inner feeling. The alignment literature answers what problem the whole project is a response to. Their internal conflicts are real: care ethics is hostile to codification, attachment theory is not established beyond human development, and the alignment literature has no native concept of a relational pattern. The paper does not paper over these conflicts; it names them and works inside them.
